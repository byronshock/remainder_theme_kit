# Remainder design system

A palette chosen for what it is far from. Every color the kit authors clears
every semantically important pole of the realized color space (§1), and the
kit's own hue is the center of the widest arc no signal claims. This file is
the authority for Remainder.

Remainder is a fork of the De Stijl theme kit and owes it everything
structural: the tier discipline, the residue doctrine, the surface list, and
above all the habit of deriving a number instead of declaring one. It parts
with De Stijl on one question, §0b.

`palette.json` is generated by `build/derive_palette.py`. Every value in this
file is read from it. Editing a color here and not there is a defect.

## 0. The strong statement

**The point is to save users from Windows**, and from any platform that
behaves like it. The skin is the smaller half of that. The larger half is
removing what the platform put in the user's way — recommendations, nags,
motion, blur — and both are in scope. A surface that ships the first without
the second is unfinished.

This half is inherited from De Stijl unchanged, and is the larger half here
too. Everything below concerns the smaller one.

### 0a. Chrome is chosen against the signals

**The hypothesis: an effective computer interface must stay away from the
semantically important poles of the realized color space.**

A user arrives at a new interface already fluent in a small vocabulary they
did not learn here. Red is an error. Yellow is a warning. Green is done. Blue
is a link you can click. Orange wants attention. That vocabulary was not
designed and cannot be repealed by a style guide. It is the accumulated
residue of every interface the user has met, and it is the first thing they
read on a surface — before the label, before the layout.

A chrome painted in those colors is therefore *always* making a claim,
whatever its designer intended. A blue titlebar says *link* somewhere below
the level the user can inspect. A yellow toolbar says *caution* faintly and
continuously, all day. The designer's insistence that the hue is composition
and not state does not reach the user; nothing in the interface carries that
footnote.

So the chrome vacates the vocabulary entirely. It takes its hue from the arc
no signal has claimed, and in exchange the signals get to mean exactly one
thing each, everywhere, with nothing else on screen competing for the
reading.

**What is left over after every pole is removed is the remainder, and that is
the kit.**

Content is exempt, and for De Stijl's reason (its §0a): a photograph, a
chart, a syntax-colored buffer is information, and information is read by its
contrast with what surrounds it. Content may use any color it likes,
including the poles — a chart of error rates *should* be red.

### 0b. Where this parts with De Stijl

De Stijl puts Mondrian's red, blue and yellow in the chrome and asks the user
to read them as composition, never state (its §8.4). Remainder holds that the
user cannot do this, and the parent kit's own machinery says so. Measured
against the poles:

| De Stijl chrome | Nearest pole | Gap | Required |
|---|---|---|---|
| blue `#1B3A6D` key titlebars | info-blue | **0.0°** — inside the arc | 10.2° |
| yellow `#DFCC82` selection | warning | **0.0°** — inside the arc | 10.2° |
| red `#D64D24` cursor, panel | destructive | 7.3° | 19.0° |

All three fail. The De Stijl titlebar is not near link blue; it *is* link
blue. This is not a defect in that kit — it is the consequence of choosing a
palette from a painting and then asking semantics to accommodate it.
Remainder chooses the palette from the semantics and lets aesthetics work in
what remains.

**The metric had to change with the question.** De Stijl measures distance in
sRGB code values, which is adequate for its own §0a (is this a *different*
color?) and useless for this one (does this *read as* a signal?). In sRGB,
`#FF6B6B` sits 127 units from FHWA red — three and a half times De Stijl's
bar — and its hue gap is 0°. It is exactly red. Lightness is not distance
from a pole. Remainder measures in OKLCh and treats a pole as an arc in hue
whose reach shrinks with chroma (§1).

### 0c. What the hypothesis does not decide

The hypothesis is a **hue constraint and nothing more**. At the home hue,
every chroma from a whisper to the gamut edge clears every pole by ~31°. The
pole test cannot choose between a quiet mauve-gray and neon pink; both are
equally far from every signal. A first derivation that maximized chroma
returned `#FD8BFF` and `#9400FB` — impeccably clear of every pole, and
unusable.

So chroma and lightness are decided on aesthetic and legibility grounds, and
this kit **labels which of its numbers are derived and which are chosen**, in
the source, next to each. Dressing a choice as a derivation is the one
dishonesty this kit cannot afford: it is exactly the move §0b accuses De
Stijl of. The chosen constants are:

| Constant | Value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| `HOME_HUE` | 351.0° | exposure, not geometry (§1) |
| `CAST` | 0.0128 | the neutrals' cast — measured, then chosen above it (§2) |
| `CHROME` | 0.1 | the chrome hues' chroma (§2) |
| `CURSOR_HUE` | 219.1° | a locator, not furniture (§2) |
| `CURSOR_CHROMA` | 0.096 | forced by the teal gamut, not chosen freely |
| `WHITE_L` | 0.93 | a field at `#FFFFFF` glares |
| `BLACK_L` | 0.15 | headroom for DARK; nearly free (§2) |
| `LIGHT_L` | 0.76 | pins the surface floor (§2) |

**Every target in this kit is a floor, and the kit sits above it on purpose.**
A derived design is only as sound as its inputs, and one input here was never
measured: that §2's "12pt system size" renders at about 16px effective. Every
contrast floor is a function of that assumption. A palette seated exactly on
its floors propagates an error in it into failure across every surface at
once, silently. Margin is what lets a derived design survive a wrong input.
Sitting at the floor would have been a choice too — just an unlabelled one,
made by the solver's rule of always taking the most extreme legal value.

### 0d. Magenta, and why there is no guard against it

Magenta signals nothing. It is not a pole, it cannot enter `poles.json`, and
avoiding it is taste — so it belongs here, in the chosen column, with its
reason. The reason turns out to be that **no angular guard is applied at
all**, and `build/field.py` shows why.

First, "magenta" names two clusters about 21° apart, and naming one angle
means picking which you meant: the **additive** RGB primary at 328.4°
(`#FF00FF`, `#DA70D6`) and the **process** ink family at 349.7–356.9°
(`#C71585`, `#EC008C`, `#FF1493`). The kit's home hue sits inside the second
range. It is not a magenta anyway, for the second reason:

Magenta is a chroma phenomenon before it is a hue one. Every named magenta
sits above chroma **0.18**. The kit authors at **0.1** and
**0.0128**, and at those levels the same hues are mauve and
dusty rose. The neon palette an early derivation returned failed on chroma
(0.196–0.292), not on hue. **The chrome ceiling is the magenta guard**, and an
angular one would buy nothing it can hold.

### 0e. The contrast metric changed too

§0b rejects a metric that answers the wrong question. WCAG 2.x contrast is
such a metric here, and the kit used it first anyway. It is a plain luminance
*ratio*, so it is polarity-blind, chroma-blind, and its `+0.05` flare constant
inflates any pair involving a near-black. Four pairs that all measured
"7.1:1 — AAA" measured **Lc 51.7 to 78.9** in APCA. Two of them — panel text
and selected-row text — were adequate only for 36px display type.

Remainder measures text contrast in **APCA Lc** (`build/apca.py`), which is
polarity-aware and signed: positive for dark text on a light field, negative
for the reverse. There is no single target. The floor is a function of
polarity, size and weight, which is the other thing a single WCAG number
hides. The kit's UI renders at about 16px effective (§2 typography):

| Lc | What it admits |
|---|---|
| 90 | body text, preferred |
| 75 | body text, minimum |
| 60 | 16px/700 bold, or 24px/400 |
| 30 | non-text marks |
| 15 | the visibility floor; nothing below it |

One consequence, recorded rather than hidden: **the kit no longer passes a
naive WCAG audit.** `WHITE` on `ACCENT` is 7.0:1, under WCAG AA for normal
text, while APCA rates it Lc 78.5 — correct for the bold titlebar text it
actually carries. The kit follows the metric that models reading.

**APCA Lc is the text metric. OKLab ΔE is the surface metric (§2).** They are
not interchangeable, and using Lc to judge whether two grounds are
distinguishable reports false collapses: two surfaces that differ in chroma
score Lc 0 while being plainly different to look at.

## 1. The poles

A pole is a region of color that a user reads as a signal before they read
anything else. The kit's pole set is **realized**, not theoretical: each
family is an arc in OKLCh hue spanned by colors actually shipped as that
signal by systems users have met. `poles.json` records the members and their
sources; `build/poles.py` derives the arcs.

| Family | Means | Hue arc |
|---|---|---|
| destructive | error, delete, discard, stop | 22.4° – 29.2° |
| caution | alert, attention, degraded | 48.1° – 62.6° |
| warning | hazard, review before proceeding | 90.4° – 109.8° |
| success | ok, done, healthy, go | 142.5° – 171.2° |
| info-blue | hyperlink, accent, selection, focus | 251.3° – 264.1° |

**info-cyan is demoted, not deleted.** Its members and measurements stay in
`poles.json` under `demoted`, with the judgement recorded beside them: the
claim is too thin to spend an arc on. Cyan means "information, note,
diagnostic" in ANSI terminals and iOS, but it is a convention of two realized
members against four each for destructive and info-blue, and unlike
red-means-error a user does not arrive already drilled on it. Restore it there
and the pole test picks it up again with no other change. The derived bars do
not move either way.

### The bars, derived from the poles themselves

Neither number is declared. Both fall out of the pole set, the way De Stijl's
`APART` falls out of its palette:

- **`C_FLOOR` = 0.092** — the chroma of the faintest realized pole (FHWA
  green). A signal shipped at that chroma is read as a signal; below it, no
  hue is doing semantic work. A color under the floor carries a cast, not a
  hue, and cannot be mistaken for anything.
- **`DELTA_MAX` = 19°** — the poles' own nearest-neighbour separation
  (destructive ends 29.2°, caution begins 48.1°). If two distinct signals sit
  18.9° apart and users tell them apart, that much is empirically enough to
  read as a different color.
- **`DELTA_REQ(C)` = `DELTA_MAX × min(1, C / C_REF)`**, `C_REF` = 0.179, the
  median realized pole chroma. Full clearance is demanded of a color as
  saturated as the signals themselves, proportionally less as it fades.

A color is **clear** if its chroma is below `C_FLOOR`, or its hue clears
every arc by `DELTA_REQ`. Chrome must be clear. Nothing else is chrome.

```
python3 build/poles.py '#773556' '#FF6B6B'
python3 build/poles.py --bars
```

### The home arc, and the guard

Five arcs leave four gaps. Three are traps: they sit *between* two signals,
and a color there reads as an ambiguous signal, which is worse than a signal.
The fourth is clear on both sides and enormous: **info-blue 264.1° →
destructive 22.4°, a 118.3° arc no signal claims.**

`DELTA_MAX` was used to guard it at first, and that was wrong. `DELTA_MAX` is
derived from how far apart two adjacent *signals* sit — a discrimination
threshold, answering "can a user tell these two apart when both are present."
A guard needs an identification boundary: "does this lone color read as that
signal." Category boundaries are wider than the spread of shipped exemplars,
and at a 19° guard the arc's own edges were `#6A69AA`, plainly a blue-violet,
and `#A1576B`, plainly a dusty red. Both passed the test. Neither should have.

**The guard is 31.4°**, taken from where the settled home hue sits relative to
destructive and applied to the blue end as well. It yields a working arc of
**295.4° – 351.0°**. Sampling the raw gap continuously (`build/sample_arc.py`)
puts the point where blue stops reading at about 295° — the same place, reached
from the other direction by a different method.

The home hue is therefore the arc's **upper edge**, not its centre. That is a
consequence of deriving the guard from where the palette already sits, and is
recorded rather than tidied away.

### The home hue is chosen, not the arc's centre

The kit authors at **351.0°**, and that is a choice (§0c).

The derived centre of the unguarded arc is 323.2°, which treats every pole as
equally entrenched. They are not. A user meets informational blue continuously
— every link, every accent, every selected row — and destructive red a few
times a week. The blue category is reinforced far harder, so the usable arc is
not centred on its own geometry: **it shifts away from blue.**

| Pole family | Clearance at 351.0° |
|---|---|
| destructive | 31.4° |
| caution | 57.1° |
| info-blue | 86.9° |
| warning | 99.4° |
| success | 151.5° |

351° is also the optimum of the band 351–353° judged acceptable aesthetically:
destructive binds across that whole band, so the minimum clearance is
maximised at the band's blue end rather than its middle.

**Recorded cost.** Moving toward red halves the tritanopic separation between
ACCENT and DESTRUCTIVE (25.4° at 323°, 13.0° at 351°). Under protanopia and
deuteranopia the two project to opposite ends of the surviving axis and
separate to ~175°, better than at 323°. Tritanopia is rare and §3 already
forbids a semantic color from carrying its meaning alone, so the cost is
accepted and written down rather than argued away.

## 2. The palette

Derived by `build/derive_palette.py` from `poles.json`. Every lightness is
solved against the floors in §0e; the chosen constants are listed in §0c.

### Neutrals — the cast

Chrome recedes. A surface a user looks at for eight hours must not be a color;
it must be a gray that remembers one. Chroma **0.0128** (chosen).

**The threshold is measured, and it is far lower than this section used to
claim.** Paired patches, each split down the middle with the cast on one side
and a true neutral at the same lightness on the other, at 0.002 steps, with a
null control at 0.000 where both halves are byte-identical. Read on this kit's
own display, 2026-09-20: the seam is **perceptible at 0.004** (ΔE 0.43) and
**clearly perceptible at 0.006** (ΔE 0.57). What stood here before — "below
about 0.014 it stops being perceptible at all" — was a judgement nobody had
tested, and it was wrong by a factor of three. It is retired to
`CONTRIBUTING.md` §9 with the rest of the failed trials.

So 0.0128 is not the least cast that can be seen; it is three times it, and the
reason is what the cast is *for*. A stronger hint that a surface is **furniture,
and not a document resting on the furniture**. Backed off from 0.016 because
that hint does not need to be as loud as it was, and stopped here rather than
lower because the hint is the point.

**0.0130 was the round number wanted and it does not derive.** The best in-gamut
teal at CURSOR's chroma reaches Lc 60.4540 against the WHITE that cast produces,
where the solver demands 60.0 plus its margin — it misses by 0.046 of an Lc
point. Reachable casts are not a range but islands, because CURSOR's floor is
read against a WHITE quantised to 8 bits; 0.0128 is the top of the island below
0.013, and it leaves CURSOR Lc 60.9 where 0.0135 would have left 60.6.

| Role | Hex | L | C | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| WHITE | `#EFE5E9` | 0.93 | 0.0120 | window backgrounds, fields, lists |
| LIGHT | `#B8AEB2` | 0.76 | 0.0127 | panels, buttons, non-key titlebars |
| DARK | `#4A4145` | 0.39 | 0.0139 | dock tiles, disabled text |
| BLACK | `#0F090C` | 0.15 | 0.0122 | rules, text, badges, the desktop field |

The four-step ladder is NeXTSTEP AppKit structure, inherited from De Stijl as
convention. **The steps are borrowed; the values are derived or declared.**

**The kit authors at 351.0° and renders within a band around it, not on the
line.** Hue is an angle taken from two small numbers, and at the cast's chroma
those numbers are small enough that rounding to 8 bits moves the angle: the four
neutrals land at 351.0°, 351.1°, 349.1°, 347.0° — a spread of 4.1°, none of it authored and none of it
avoidable while the output is sRGB. It costs nothing. Every one of them is far
under `C_FLOOR`, so the pole test passes them as carrying no readable hue and
asks no clearance of them at all; the clearances reported for them move by a
degree or two between casts for this reason alone, and reading that as drift is
reading quantisation as intent. The three chrome hues carry enough chroma that
the same rounding moves them by a few tenths.

`WHITE_L` and `LIGHT_L` are both pinned, and that is deliberate: the surface
floor below is derived from WHITE against LIGHT, so a floating LIGHT means a
floating bar. It moved once already — darkening BLACK shifted LIGHT by ΔE 1.5,
invisible in itself, and moved the floor underneath every value measured
against it. A bar that moves when something else moves is not a bar.

`BLACK_L` is chosen at 0.15 rather than solved. Solved, it sat at L 0.21 and
squeezed DARK into a corridor 0.076 wide between the rule below it and its own
text floor above. APCA soft-clamps the black end — below a threshold, darker
text buys almost nothing — so darkening BLACK is nearly free: it *raised*
BLACK on WHITE from 90.6 to 91.8 while roughly doubling DARK's margins.
The cost is headroom against pure `#000000`, which §3 reserves for legend:
ΔE 20.9 → **15.0**. Still clearly short of it, and spent deliberately.

DARK is solved differently from the rest: it is pulled lighter so the BLACK
rule reads against it, and darker for its own white labels. It takes the point
where neither margin is nearer failing than the other.

### Chrome hues

The two things that must be *noticed* — the key titlebar and the selected row
— are allowed a real hue. Chroma **0.1** (chosen): just above `C_FLOOR`, so
they are honestly hue-bearing and must pass the pole test rather than escape
it as neutrals; and far below a signal's own chroma (0.179), because this kit's
accent is not competing with an error dialog for attention.

| Role | Hex | L | C | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ACCENT | `#773556` | 0.43 | 0.1007 | key titlebars, links, toggles, nav indicators, focus |
| SELECT | `#531537` | 0.31 | 0.0993 | selected rows and selected text, WHITE on it |

**Selection is a dark ground carrying light text, not a pale tint.** A pale
selection cannot do both jobs at this chroma: every tint light enough to carry
body text at Lc 75 is invisible against the WHITE field. The best available
reached Lc 73.7 at ΔE 15.1 separation, and failed both ways at once.

ACCENT and SELECT are placed to maximise legibility subject to staying
distinguishable, and sit essentially on that frontier — within 0.6 Lc of the
best available at their separation. Their own boundary is exempt from the
surface floor below, because a selected topmost row abutting the titlebar
still reads: it is ΔE 46 from the unselected rows that surround it, which is
what communicates "selected." A soft edge against the titlebar hides nothing.

### The cursor is the exception: a second hue

| Role | Hex | L | C | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CURSOR | `#007891` | 0.53 | 0.0958 | text cursor indicator, terminal cursor, the key window's frame (§5) |

ACCENT, SELECT and the neutrals are furniture — read once, then unseen. A text
cursor is a **locator**: its whole purpose is to be found fast, repeatedly, on
a field of text. Solved at the home hue it came out within 3 Lc and 0.03 L of
ACCENT — two roles, one color, and the one that needed to stand out did not.
This is De Stijl §4b's own test: if removing the color makes the thing harder
to *find*, the color is information.

219.1° sits in the arc that opens once info-cyan is demoted, 131.9° from the
home hue — as far from the rest of the kit as the pole set allows. Its chroma
is **0.096**, forced rather than chosen: sRGB has no dark saturated teal, and
at the lightness Lc 60 demands that is the gamut ceiling. It clears `C_FLOOR`
by 0.004, so the cursor is still honestly hue-bearing — but that is the whole
margin there is. **It is the least robust value in the kit**, living on three
simultaneous margins: +0.004 chroma, 0.8° inside the guard edge, +0.7 Lc.

**CURSOR is the one hue the kit assigns to a state, and the state is *where
input goes*.** In a field of text that is the caret. On a screen of windows it
is the key window, and the kit marks it with the same hue: a CURSOR band drawn
in the rule beside the key window (§5). There is one caret and one key window,
so the locator hue is never on screen twice for two reasons — the frame and
the caret inside it are one statement at two scales. Nothing about this makes
CURSOR a ground: WHITE on it is Lc −64.9 and BLACK on it 29.1, under every
text tier, so it marks a key window and never paints its titlebar. As a mark it
is read against the field it bounds — Lc 60.9 on WHITE, 30.3 on LIGHT, both
over APCA's 30 for a mark — and it is recorded, not claimed, against the BLACK
rule on its other side: Lc −26.8, under that tier by 3.2, which is the side
the eye is not on.

### Contrast, measured

| Pair | Lc | Floor | Margin | Carries |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| BLACK on WHITE | 91.9 | 90 | +1.9 | body text on the window field, 16px/400 |
| BLACK on LIGHT | 61.3 | 60 | +1.3 | panel and button text, 16px/700 bold (§2) |
| WHITE on DARK | -81.7 | 75 | +6.7 | dock tile labels, 16px/400 |
| WHITE on ACCENT | -78.5 | 60 | +18.5 | titlebar text, 16px/700 bold (§2) |
| WHITE on SELECT | -87.5 | 75 | +12.5 | text on a selected row, 16px/400 |
| CURSOR on WHITE | 60.9 | 60 | +0.9 | the text cursor: a mark, wants to be seen |

Solved with a margin so each pair still clears after rounding to 8 bits.
Three pairs sit near their floor for structural reasons, not by choice: LIGHT
is pinned, CURSOR is capped by the teal gamut, and BLACK on WHITE is already
at APCA's preferred tier.

### Surfaces must be distinguishable

Text legibility is not enough: a user must also see that two adjacent surfaces
*are* two surfaces. That is a different question and takes a different metric —
**OKLab ΔE**, not APCA Lc (§0e).

The floor is derived, not declared. It is the weakest boundary the kit already
relies on to divide two surfaces where no rule is drawn — WHITE against LIGHT,
which §5 names as a separator in its own right. Anything that must read, must
read at least that well.

**`SURFACE_FLOOR` = ΔE 17.1.** Same shape as De Stijl's `APART`: the weakest
member sets the bar and meets it exactly, so WHITE/LIGHT has no margin on it
by construction. Both its endpoints are pinned so the bar cannot drift.

Load-bearing boundaries, weakest first:

| Pair | ΔE |
|---|---|
| WHITE / LIGHT | 17.1 |
| BLACK / DARK | 23.6 |
| LIGHT / ACCENT | 34.4 |
| LIGHT / DARK | 37.4 |
| WHITE / CURSOR | 41.5 |

The remaining 6 clear by 30 or more.

Four pairs are **exempt**, and for a reason rather than by exception. §5 draws
a rule *outside* the field it bounds, so what a rule must distinguish is
rule-against-field — BLACK/WHITE at ΔE 78.3, BLACK/LIGHT at 61.2.
Rule-against-chrome at a window's edge is not its job, and where the rule meets
the desktop the two are now the same value by construction (§5): the gap *is*
the rule, so a window's edge reads as its own field against the surround rather
than as a line drawn on it.

| Exempt pair | ΔE | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ACCENT / SELECT | 11.7 | selection reads against the rows around it, not the titlebar above |
| SELECT / BLACK | 18.4 | rule meeting chrome at a window edge |
| ACCENT / BLACK | 29.2 | rule meeting chrome at a window edge |
| DARK / SELECT | 11.3 | they do not touch |

## 3. Semantic state — the axiom

In De Stijl the three signal colors are an exception carved out of the palette
(its §1b). In Remainder they are the **axiom the palette is a consequence
of**, and they are the only hues in the system that mean anything.

| Role | Hex | FHWA color | Legend |
|---|---|---|---|
| SUCCESS | `#006B54` | Green, Pantone 342 | `#FFFFFF` |
| WARNING | `#FCD116` | Yellow, Pantone 116 | `#000000` |
| DESTRUCTIVE | `#AF1E2D` | Red, Pantone 187 | `#FFFFFF` |

Unchanged from De Stijl. They appear **only when the meaning is present** — and
in this kit that rule finally costs nothing, because no chrome anywhere is
competing with them. A red button that isn't destructive is a defect.

`#FFFFFF` and `#000000` are **reserved for legend and refused anywhere else**,
and `build/poles.py` enforces it (`--legend` admits them for the one permitted
use). The pole test cannot catch them by measurement — both have zero chroma
and pass as neutrals — so they are reserved by rule, for two reasons. They are
the legend, and a legend that also appears as chrome stops being a legend. And
`#FFFFFF` is what an *unstyled* surface renders: a field painted with it cannot
be told apart from a theme that failed to load, which is a signal the kit did
not intend to send.

ACCENT and DESTRUCTIVE measure **Lc 0.0** against each other — identical
luminance, separated by 31° of hue alone, and that is true at any home hue
the kit could have picked. A destructive button carries its label as well as
its color, which is what §3 requires of it.

Hyperlinks are the one real loss. Link blue is the most entrenched color
meaning in computing and Remainder gives it up, routing links to ACCENT. This
is the hypothesis's largest bill and the kit pays it rather than carve an
exception; a link is still underlined, and underline is what the convention
actually rests on.

## 4. Inherited from De Stijl, unchanged

Carried verbatim, and not re-argued here. See `DESTIJL_STYLE.md` in the
parent kit:

- **§0 removal** — the declutter work, the larger half (above).
- **Residue doctrine** — the kit authors none; where proprietary software
  imposes one the kit cannot reach, it is tolerated, never echoed.
- **worksafe / elevated tiers** — the boundary is binding.
- **Surface findings** — which registry key, which pref, what COSMIC will and
  will not let a theme reach. Kept in `PLATFORM.md`, which is theme-
  independent and parallel in both kits.
- **Application icons** — a brand is information and keeps its own art, so
  **the kit repaints nothing by default**. *(The difference from De Stijl is
  when, not whether: its §4b ships the projection with the install, Remainder
  makes it a choice. That §4b described a kit that does not repaint while its
  COSMIC surface repainted, from 2026-09-08 until the rule was written down on
  2026-09-19 — its own issue 006 had asked for the sentence and it went
  unwritten for eleven days.)*

  **The user may decide otherwise, and the kit gives them the means.** A brand
  is information to someone who wants to be told whose software this is, and
  furniture to someone who already knows and wants a dock that reads as one
  surface. Which it is on a given screen is that user's judgement, not the
  kit's. `build/icon_theme.py` repaints the icons that machine actually has and
  writes an overlay theme. It is opt-in (`install.sh --icons`), reversible by
  selecting another icon theme, and never committed: the output is the user's
  own brands in the user's own paint, and the kit has no license to redistribute
  anybody's brand art. The generator authors nothing — an icon the chain cannot
  find is skipped, not invented.

  **What the repaint keeps is lightness.** Every pixel holds its own lightness
  and takes its hue and chroma from that lightness, along a ramp whose knots are
  values §2 authors, ordered by L: BLACK → SELECT → ACCENT → LIGHT → WHITE.
  Neutral at both ends, the home hue through the middle — a duotone print in the
  kit's own ink. The shape survives, the shading survives, and which icon is
  lighter than which survives, which is most of what a dock is read by.

  **This is where an icon stops being made of authored values, and that is a
  claim being withdrawn.** Until 2026-09-19 the generator sent each pixel to the
  *nearest* of the seven, flat, so every pixel in a repainted icon was a value
  this document names. It was a claim about the output that cost the thing icons
  are for. Over six of this machine's own icons it held per-pixel lightness at
  r 0.906 and its *ordering* at ρ 0.759 — a quarter of the light-and-dark inside
  every icon reassigned — and 25.3% of the opaque pixels came back CURSOR teal,
  spending across a whole dock the one hue §2 keeps for a locator. The ramp
  gives r 0.997 and ρ 0.994, and reaches CURSOR never.

  What is left is the bar §1 actually sets, and it is narrower rather than
  weaker: **every value the ramp can emit clears every pole, and none is
  reserved (§3).** The ramp passes through exactly 667 distinct 8-bit values,
  five of them authored; it terminates at BLACK and WHITE rather than passing
  through them, which is what keeps `#000000` and `#FFFFFF` off a repainted
  icon. `python3 build/remainder_space.py --check` sweeps the ramp and reports
  all of it, so this is measured and not asserted (§6.10, §6.11).
- **Modern rendering** — anti-aliased type, 8-bit icons, real shadows,
  resolution-independent points, integer-scaled.

## 5. Inherited as convention, and labelled

Remainder has no source artifact. De Stijl derives its rule width, its
wallpaper, and its argument for Helvetica from the 1922 composition. Strip the
painting and those derivations have no source. Remainder therefore **declares**
them rather than pretending to measure them:

- **Geometry.** One rule, BLACK, drawn outside the field it bounds, the same
  everywhere on a screen, carrying no state. Reference width 22 density-independent pixels (dp/pt) , a 44 pt, 1 cm effective hit box, taken from the sizing guidelines by input modality according to Fitt's Law to enable touch surfaces. References will follow.

  There is no thinner rule. **A separator that wants to be lighter than the
  rule is not a rule; it is a change of field tone — WHITE against LIGHT — and
  draws no line.** That tone change is a boundary the kit relies on to divide
  two surfaces with nothing drawn between them, which is why §2 takes it as
  the floor every other load-bearing boundary must clear.

  **The key window is marked in the rule, not by it.** Beside the key window
  the innermost **8 dp** of the rule's width is CURSOR (§2), drawn from the
  window's edge outward, and the remaining 14 dp is the rule as everywhere
  else — the window does not move, the gap does not change width, the hit box
  holds. A mark drawn in the rule, as the caret is drawn in the text. The 8 is
  chosen (§0c): narrower than the rule so the rule survives beside it and still
  reads as one rule around every window, and a whole number of device pixels
  at every quarter-step scale, so the mark's edge is never a blended value.
  22 — the rule's own width — was tried and looked at on 2026-09-21: the key
  window's rule turned CURSOR entirely and touched the panel and its
  neighbours, which is the rule carrying state, and this section does not
  allow it. Measured on COSMIC at 150% the same day: 12 px, every pixel
  `#007891` exactly, drawn in the gap and not over the window (`PLATFORM.md`).
- **Typography.** UI Montserrat, mono Hack, 12pt system
  size. **This is load-bearing and unmeasured**: every contrast floor in §0e is
  a function of what "12pt" renders at, taken to be about 16px effective. If
  that is wrong, every floor moves. It is the kit's weakest input and the
  reason §0c insists on margin.

  **The cuts are Medium and ExtraBold**, not Regular and Bold, and the reason is
  the same margin. Montserrat Regular reads thin at 16px, and weight is the one
  input here that is *safe in one direction only*: every floor in the kit gets
  easier as the rendered weight goes up and harder as it comes down. The pair
  with nothing to lose is BLACK on LIGHT at Lc 61.2 — +1.2 over APCA's 16px/700
  tier, on a ground §2 pins because `SURFACE_FLOOR` is derived from it — so a
  lighter bold could not be paid for by moving a colour. Both cuts moved the
  safe way: 400 → 500 and 700 → 800. The stylesheets still declare 400 and 700
  and are untouched; CSS font matching resolves those to the installed 500 and
  800, which keeps the declared weight the one the checkers measure against and
  makes the rendered weight heavier than that floor assumes. Choosing the other
  direction would have made `build/firefox.py`'s weight-aware floor a false
  pass, which is worse than a failing one. Verified on the running chrome,
  2026-09-19: a tab label at declared 700 renders Montserrat ExtraBold and the
  toolbar at 400 renders Montserrat Medium.
- **Wallpaper.** No painting, so no composed desktop. The field is a flat
  **BLACK** one. The tiling gaps are the rule, and at BLACK they are
  literally it: the rule is BLACK, so the gap between two windows is the same
  value as the line the kit draws — ΔE 0.0, where a DARK field left the two
  ΔE 23.7 apart and made the claim true only by approximation. Beside the key
  window the innermost 8 dp of that gap is the CURSOR mark (Geometry, above)
  and the other 14 dp is still the rule. It also buys the
  desktop's own labels margin, WHITE on BLACK measuring Lc −92.3 against −81.7
  on DARK. Dock tiles stay DARK and read against the field at ΔE 23.7, clear of
  the ΔE 17.1 floor. Photographs are content (§0a) and are permitted; nothing
  the kit generates is in color.

  The installer's default background is one such photograph, fitted on the
  field rather than covering it, so the surround and the tiling gaps are still
  BLACK and everything above still holds of them. That default is a choice
  about what a desktop should look like and not a measurement, and it has a
  price this section will not hide: over the photograph's light studio ground
  the desktop's own labels fall to Lc −35.9 at the median, under every floor in
  §0e. It is the one place the kit knowingly ships under one. The installer
  states the number in the question that offers it, and the flat field is the
  second answer.

## 6. Principles

1. Chrome clears every pole of the realized color space (§1). Any value that
   does not is a defect, platform residue excepted.
2. The poles are realized, not theoretical: measured from colors systems
   actually ship as signals.
3. The bars are derived from the pole set, never declared.
4. The hypothesis constrains hue only. Chroma and lightness are aesthetic and
   legibility choices, and every one of them is labelled as a choice (§0c).
5. Every target is a floor, and the kit sits above it on purpose (§0c).
6. Text legibility is APCA Lc; surface distinguishability is OKLab ΔE. They
   are not interchangeable (§0e, §2).
7. A boundary that must read, must read at least as well as the separator the
   kit already trusts to do that job (§2).
8. State is carried by the three semantic hues (§3) when the meaning is
   present; *where input goes* — the caret in a field, the key window on a
   screen — by CURSOR, the one hue the kit assigns to a state (§2); and
   otherwise by tone and geometry. No chrome hue means anything.
9. Content is exempt and may use any color, poles included (§0a).
10. Measure, don't eyeball.
11. Deriving a number beats declaring one; declaring one openly beats dressing
    it as a derivation.
12. QA every build visually before delivery.
13. `palette.json` is generated. Edit the poles or the derivation, never the
    output — and never a color in this file alone.
14. `CONTRIBUTING.md` is the procedure, and every rule in it is binding.
